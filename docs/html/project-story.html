Boardy AI · Intro Matching
How the project unfolded
Recommender Systems · Boardy AI

Can we tell which introductions people will actually accept?

Two weeks of work, in the order it happened.

July 16 — July 30, 2026
01  ·  July 16

We started with two hundred real introductions.

A hundred people said yes. A hundred said no. Every single one had already been recommended by Boardy's live system.

The decision

Learn from what the humans did, not from what the system suggested.

02  ·  July 17

First we measured what existing models could already do.

General-purpose language models were barely better than guessing. Boardy's own family of models did meaningfully better.

The decision

Nothing gets called an improvement until it beats these numbers.

03  ·  July 18

Two hundred examples is not much to learn from.

So we built a pipeline that writes realistic new people and new introductions, and left five hundred of them generating overnight.

The decision

We cannot buy more real outcomes, so we will manufacture practice ones.

04  ·  July 19

The first trained model looked brilliant. It wasn't.

Near-perfect on the data we invented. Worse than a coin flip on the real cases that actually matter.

The decision

Stop everything and find out what it really learned.

05  ·  July 19, later

It had learned our writing style, not human behaviour.

Our generator described good matches and bad matches differently. The model simply spotted the difference in the prose.

The decision

Throw the batch away. A model trained only on real pairs beat it using a fifth of the data.

06  ·  July 20

The flaw was that the writer knew the answer in advance.

So we split the job in two: invent the people first, decide whether they are a good match afterwards.

The decision

Never let the answer touch the text.

07  ·  July 21

We asked whether these people form a network we could learn from.

They mostly don't. Almost everybody appears exactly once, so there is very little structure to exploit.

The decision

Park the graph-based ideas until there is an actual graph.

08  ·  July 22

A knowledge-graph approach would need a different kind of data.

Not more examples — thousands of carefully edited variations of the same people, to show the model what changes an outcome.

The decision

Understood as a research direction, not a next step.

09  ·  July 23

One person asks for funding, hiring and advice in the same breath.

Blending all of it into a single summary quietly loses all three requests at once.

The decision

Read each request separately, then match each one on its own.

10  ·  July 24

We finally wrote down the real question.

Not "are these two relevant to each other" — the live system already decided that. It is whether two plausible strangers will genuinely connect.

The turning point

And a free, open model beat Boardy's production model at answering it.

11  ·  July 25

We rebuilt data generation so nothing marks its own homework.

One system shortlists candidates. A completely different one decides yes or no. They never share a model or a method.

The decision

Keep the finder and the judge strictly apart.

12  ·  July 26

A thousand invented people. Twenty-six hundred judgements.

About six dollars of compute — and kept in its own folder, never mixed into the real data.

The decision

Scale the practice data, but keep it quarantined from the truth.

13  ·  July 27

We turned the work into a proposal rather than a report.

Less about everything we tried, more about what should be built next and why.

The decision

Lead with the recommendation, keep the experiments as evidence.

14  ·  July 28

Teach by comparison, and show more comparisons at once.

One person, one good match, one bad match, many at a time. That finally beat the production model. The mixture-of-experts idea did not — it learned our judge's opinions instead of real human choices.

The decision

Train on comparisons, not on isolated pairs.

15  ·  July 29

Then we checked whether the wins were real.

Scored against all two hundred pairs instead of the small set held back, some gains shrank into noise. What held up was performance on the hard cases.

The decision

From now on, only the hard cases count — they are the ones production gets wrong.

Where it stands

Two things proven. One still open.

A free, open model beats the production one.
On the question that matters: will these two people actually connect.
Teaching by comparison works.
And it runs fast enough to serve, which the accurate-but-slow options do not.
Invented data still doesn't teach real outcomes.
It teaches whichever model wrote the labels. That is the thread left to pull.
