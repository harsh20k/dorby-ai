Fine-tuning learned to judge a match better — and got worse at finding it in a crowd

We LoRA fine-tuned two embedding models — voyage-4-nano and Qwen3-Embedding-8B — on 1,056 synthetic training examples, using a training signal (triplet loss) never tried before in this project. Both beat the frozen production model at telling a good intro from a bad one. Neither beats it at picking the single best match out of a crowd. Here's the training data, the loss curves, and the plain-language reason those two things pulled apart.

01

What we actually did, in plain words

No jargon left unexplained. If you only read one section, read this one.

1
Start from a frozen embedding model. An embedding model turns a person's profile into a list of numbers (a vector) such that similar profiles land near each other in that number-space. Boardy's real production model is Voyage-4-large, made by a company called Voyage AI. We tested it, plus a free, smaller open-weight model called Qwen3-Embedding-8B, made by Alibaba's Qwen team.
2
Generate synthetic training examples (batch rrf_003). We don't have enough real accept/decline outcomes to fine-tune on (200 total). So we generated fictional user profiles, had an AI write a realistic "who I'm looking for" query for each one, used two different search methods to find candidate matches for each query, and had a separate judge AI (gemini-3.1-flash-lite) decide, one pair at a time, whether each candidate was actually a good intro. This produced 1,059 labeled query/candidate outcomes.
3
Turn labels into triplets. A triplet is three things bundled together: an anchor (the seeker's query), a positive (a candidate the judge approved), and a negative (a candidate the judge rejected for that same query). We only kept seekers where the judge had given us at least one of each — 385 such seekers — and paired every positive with every negative they had, for 1,056 triplets total.
4
Fine-tune with LoRA + a "ranking" loss. Instead of retraining the whole model (slow, expensive, and prone to forgetting everything it already knew), LoRA bolts on a small number of new, trainable weights — about 0.1–0.3% of the model — and leaves the rest frozen. We trained those small weights with MultipleNegativesRankingLoss: for every triplet, nudge the anchor's vector closer to its positive and farther from its negative, all at once, in one step.
5
Score on real outcomes only. All of the training data above is a judge's opinion, not a real accept/decline. So every number in this report is measured against the 69 held-out real pairs — actual intros Boardy's production system made, where a human either followed up or didn't. This is the only population that decides whether any of this matters.
Why this run is separate from earlier ones

Two earlier fine-tuning attempts in this project (run_001, arm_a_real_only) used a different loss (ContrastiveLoss, which just needs pass/fail pairs, not matched triplets) because at the time almost no seeker had both an approved and a rejected candidate to pair up. The RRF pipeline that produced rrf_003 fixed that — 385 of its seekers have both — which is what finally made triplet training possible.

The headline

Qwen3-Embedding-8B's triplet fine-tune is now the best pair AUC of any model or arm tried in this project — including its own frozen (untuned) self, and Boardy's actual production model. Both fine-tunes beat Voyage-4-large on classification. Neither beats it on retrieval.

04

Why did accuracy go up while retrieval went down?

This looks like a contradiction. It isn't — the two numbers are answering different questions.

Pair AUC — "is this pair good or bad?"

Shown one seeker and one candidate at a time, can the model correctly rank the ones that were actually accepted above the ones that were declined? This is a head-to-head comparison — the model only ever has to beat the specific alternative it's shown.

Retrieval recall@1 — "find the needle in the haystack"

Shown one seeker's query and the entire pool of ~40+ candidates in the holdout, does the one true match come out ranked #1 by embedding similarity — ahead of every other person in the pool, most of whom aren't even related to what the seeker is asking for?

Fine-tuning on triplets only ever showed the model a small, curated slice of that full haystack at a time — one anchor, one accepted candidate, one declined candidate. MultipleNegativesRankingLoss also borrows other examples in the same training batch as extra "negatives," but that's still a handful, not the whole holdout pool. The model got very good at the narrow comparison it was actually trained on — this candidate vs. that specific alternative — which is exactly what pair AUC measures.

What it wasn't directly trained to preserve is the broader property Voyage-4-large's much larger, much more general pretraining already had baked in: keep every unrelated profile far away in the same shared vector space, not just the one alternative it happened to see during training. That's precisely what recall@1 needs — the true match has to outrank everyone, not just one runner-up. A few thousand fine-tuning steps on curated pairs is a much narrower signal than the vast, general-purpose training that produced Voyage-4-large in the first place, so it's unsurprising that broad ranking-over-everyone erodes a bit even as the narrow judgment sharpens.

The one place this doesn't hold

At recall@10 — is the true match anywhere in the top 10, not just #1 — Qwen3-8B's fine-tune ties Voyage-4-large exactly (0.862 vs 0.862), despite trailing badly at #1. Read plainly: fine-tuning didn't push the right answer out of contention, it just made the model less confident about which single candidate to lead with. That's consistent with a product that shows a shortlist rather than one committed pick.

05

What not to conclude from this

  • Every training label here is an LLM judge's opinion, not a real outcome. The 69-pair holdout used for every number above is real; the 1,056 triplets used to train are not — treat the fine-tune as trained on a proxy for the real task, not the real task itself.
  • The Qwen3-8B run's checkpoint-selection may be leaving accuracy on the table. The training log shows its best dev-set epoch (epoch 2, 71.1% triplet accuracy) had its checkpoint auto-deleted before final model selection ran (a storage-limit setting kept only the 3 most recent checkpoints), so the final model actually shipped is the last epoch (5), not the best one seen during training.
  • Sample size is small — 69 holdout pairs, 29 positive queries. Individual metric swings of a few points are within noise.
  • Neither model has a latency benchmark yet against the project's <100ms serving budget; these are offline accuracy numbers only.
