Reciprocity · plain-language explainer

Does this person want you back?

Every model in this project checked one direction. The other direction turned out to matter more.

The idea in one sentence

An intro involves two people, and both have to want it — but every model here only ever checked one direction.

Until now the question was always: does this candidate match what the seeker asked for? Nobody asked the other half: does the seeker match what the candidate is looking for?

Why that matters

The knowledge-graph experiment caught a perfect example. Adrian searched for a proptech investor. Kunle's profile hit every keyword — proptech, real estate, investor. One-direction check: great match. Adrian declined.

Reason: Kunle wasn't an investor. He was out raising money himself. Two people both looking for a check, neither holding one. Checking only Adrian's side made it look great. Checking Kunle's side would have killed it instantly.

How it's built

Each person's profile gets split into two piles:

Pile 1

What I want

Their lookingFor section — plus their search query, if they're the one doing the searching.

Pile 2

What I am

Everything else: background, positioning, notes, location, preferences.

Then you compute two scores instead of one:

  • Forward: does what the seeker wants line up with what the candidate is?
  • Reverse: does what the candidate wants line up with what the seeker is?

Final score = forward + λ × reverse, where λ is a single number tuned on the training pairs only.

The interesting part

1.75
That's what the tuned number came out at. The reverse direction ended up weighted almost twice as heavily as the forward one.

Which is a striking thing to find, because the forward direction is the only one anybody has ever measured. If it holds up, "does this person want you back" matters more than "does this person match your search."

No training whatsoever — a frozen off-the-shelf model, one number tuned. It improved on all three groups tested.

But this didn't beat the pack — it's a promising lead

When you resample the data to check how solid the gain is, the range of plausible answers still includes "no gain at all" — roughly 76% and 84% likely to be real on the two groups. At 200 pairs you can't tell the difference. What makes it worth taking seriously isn't any single number, it's that three separate groups all moved the same way for essentially zero cost.

Two more things worth knowing

It helps with judging, not finding

Retrieval numbers are unchanged, deliberately. You can't search your index by "who wants me" — that would mean re-ranking everyone on every query. It's a second-pass filter over a shortlist, which fits exactly the two-stage shape this project keeps arriving at.

This is the simplest possible version of the idea

The full design in the professor's paper learns from a user's history — what got shown, what got clicked, in what order. This data has none of that, just one static accept/decline per pair. The paper's own math says that with no history, it reduces to precisely what was built here. So this is the floor of the idea, not the ceiling.

The experiments behind this

Four separate pieces of work, in plain words.

1. The reciprocity run itself

A frozen, off-the-shelf embedding model — nothing trained, nothing fine-tuned. Every profile was cut into the two piles described above, and both scores were computed for every pair.

The one number that needed choosing, λ, was picked by trying every value from −2 to 2 in steps of 0.05 and keeping whichever scored best on the 131 training pairs only. The holdout pairs were never looked at while choosing it. On the training pairs the score went from 0.5512 to 0.5944; the best value was 1.75.

Then that fixed number was applied, untouched, to two groups it had never seen: the 69 holdout pairs and the full 200. Both improved. Total cost was a few minutes on a rented GPU.

The honesty check: the data was resampled 5,000 times to see how much of the gain could just be luck. The answer was that the gain is probably real but not provably real — the plausible range still touches zero on both groups. That's a limit of having 200 pairs, not a flaw in the method.

2. The knowledge-graph experiment (where the Adrian case came from)

Three profiles, three LLM calls, no training. One real person appeared in two different real pairs with opposite outcomes — accepted in one, declined in the other. Each profile was turned into a small map of facts: their role, their industry, what they need, where they are.

The maps were merged, and one concept turned out to touch all three people: proptech. Same shared topic, opposite real outcomes. The declined pair had more words in common than the accepted one.

Reading the actual profile text explained why, and this is the part that matters: the accepted match had weaker keyword overlap but the two roles fit together — one wanted peers and mentoring, the other could be a peer and mentor. The declined match had perfect keyword overlap and two people standing on the same side of the table. That's the failure this whole reciprocity idea is built to catch.

3. The search-query sweep (why one direction isn't enough)

A separate experiment turned a dial controlling how much the search query counted versus the rest of a person's profile. Turning it up made finding the right person dramatically better — the right person landed in the top ten 91% of the time instead of 59%.

But it barely moved predicting whether they'd say yes. That score went from 0.559 to 0.587 and then back down again.

That's the finding that makes reciprocity interesting. Better one-direction matching solves the finding problem and leaves the accepting problem untouched. So the missing signal isn't "match harder" — it's something one-direction matching structurally cannot see.

4. The wall everything else hit

Every model tried in this project — off-the-shelf ones, the production model, and every fine-tuned version — scores between roughly 0.55 and 0.60 at telling accept from decline. Fine-tuning didn't break out of it. A mixture-of-experts design didn't break out of it, and its own results concluded the problem was the data, not the design.

Two things have crept above that band. One is an LLM that reads both profiles in full and is never shown the search query at all. The other is this reciprocity term. Both are about whether two people fit together, not about whether their words look alike. That's the pattern worth following.

What hasn't been done yet

  • Both piles are read by the same frozen model. Training a separate one for each is untested.
  • λ was fitted once. Fitting it repeatedly on different slices of the training data would show how stable 1.75 really is.
  • The combination is deliberately simple — forward plus a multiple of reverse. A slightly more flexible formula is cheap and untried.
  • Most importantly: 200 pairs is too few to settle any of this. More real labelled outcomes would do more than any new architecture.
